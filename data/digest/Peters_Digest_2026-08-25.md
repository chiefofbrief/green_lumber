**Glossary**

**Growth**
- **TTM Sales Growth** — Trailing-twelve-month revenue vs. the prior twelve-month period, % change.
- **Qtrly Sales Growth** — Most recent quarter's revenue vs. the same quarter one year earlier (year-over-year), % change.
- **TTM Accel** — Change in the annual growth rate: this period's TTM sales growth minus the prior period's TTM sales growth, in percentage points. Positive means growth is speeding up.
- **Qtrly Accel** — Change in the quarterly growth rate: the latest quarter's year-over-year growth minus the year-over-year growth from four quarters earlier, in percentage points.

**Profitability**
- **Gross Margin** — Trailing-twelve-month gross profit as a percentage of trailing-twelve-month sales.
- **FCF/Sales** — Trailing-twelve-month free cash flow as a percentage of trailing-twelve-month sales.

**Affordability**
- **EV/Sales** — Enterprise value (market cap + total debt − cash) divided by trailing-twelve-month sales. A valuation multiple — lower generally means cheaper.
- **P/E** — Market cap divided by trailing-twelve-month net income. Blank when net income is zero or negative.

It's hard to know where to look first: the _Looney Tunes_ -ass slapstick of a bipedal robot completely wiping out on the athletics track; the pure awe of another bot casually shattering Usain Bolt's 100m record; or the low-key shock of others going full _Jetsons_ backstage by quietly microwaving snacks and routing cables for judges.

But no matter where you look, you're _looking_, which is kinda the point of China's **second-ever World Humanoid Robot Games** now underway in Beijing.

**First**, take a peek beyond all the virality at **the organisers** : we're talking the Beijing Municipality, the China Media Group (the state's primary propaganda arm), and the Beijing-backed-and-hosted World Robot Cooperation Organization (aiming to set the world's robot standards like Western groups did for the internet).

Or to put it another way: while this might look like just another neutral tech showcase chasing clicks and bucks (and yes, it is clearly that too), it's really more interesting viewed as a window into **China's deeper strategy for the future**.

And from our vantage, it's hereby sending **three big messages**, starting with...

 1. 🤖 **The future is robotic**




Take a look not so much at this year's event, but rather at the ways it's evolved **compared to last year** : yes, a doubling in teams and events, and a quadrupling in the number of robots (over 2,000).

But more intriguingly, **the rules just got tougher** : remote control is now more restricted or banned, forcing more of the bots to handle balance, navigation, and recovery fully on-chip.

And even more intriguingly, the events just got **more industrial** : 21 of this year's 51 events are purely "_scenarios_ " testing micro-dexterity like wiring, sorting, restaurant service, and even operating household appliances.

Or to put it another way, yes, in a single year we've gone from "_can this robot finish the race_ " to "_omg that robot just beat Usain Bolt_ ". But that's just the consumer-facing marketing layer. The**deeper message** is we just went from remote-controlled parlour tricks to autonomous, high-precision industrial labour in a year.

And yet this event doesn't just paint a picture of the future, so much as declare...

 2. 🇨🇳 **That future is China's**




641 of the 666 competing teams — and ~2,000 of their 2,056 robots — hail from China. Yes, that's partly because the West’s leaders (think Figure, Tesla, Boston) aren’t competing, presumably out of commercial caution, competitive secrecy, and discomfort with political theatre. The rest of the teams hailed from **16 nations** (including the US, Germany, Japan, Brazil), but were mostly university and smaller commercial players.

Yet even with that throat-clearing out of the way, China's 96% domination of its own robot games now roughly matches China's dominance over **global humanoid robot shipments**. The big two (AgiBot, Unitree) alone now ship ~75% of all bots, while the US does ~3%.

The caveat is that shipments aren’t the same as productive deployment, many of those shipments are still early pilots, and the Games themselves expose other technical gaps (like how to… stop).

But it's not just end-product domination — from harmonic reducers and high-torque actuators to specialised batteries and frameless motors, China has now localised almost the **entire supply chain**. Or to put it another way, it's getting hard for anyone else to build a bot without relying on China-built parts, and all the scale, cost, and iteration advantages that accrue to China along the way.

Yes, the West still holds an edge in raw brains (AI models), but those brains increasingly run (literally) on China's hardware. Anyway, if any of this feels familiar, it's because you've already seen the two prequels, bringing us to...

 3. ⚡ **The China Shock 3.0**




If the first state-backed China Shock™ (2000s) was about dominating low-cost consumer goods, and the second (2015-2020s) has hit green tech's _New Three_ (EVs, solar, batteries), then this next China Shock 3.0 is really about shaping **the AI age**.

We've long explored the risks China's high-quality and low-cost open models now pose to the founding assumptions of **America's entire AI boom** : the notion that closed models will preserve Silicon Valley's moat, guarantee monopoly pricing, require all this historic Big Tech capex, and justify Wall Street's historic valuations.

But these robot games are a reminder that China is not only focused on AI models, nor even breaking China’s reliance on the US-aligned chips underneath.

Rather, we're really seeing here a reminder that China is also racing just as hard to dominate the newer, physical half of this AI age: embodied intelligence.

#### Intrigue’s Take

China has been relatively open about both how it got here, and why — but with one intriguing exception.

**First**, on the **how** : it’s all laid out in Beijing's big 2015 'Made in China' strategy and follow-up docs, like the ruling Party's five-year plans, its 2021 _Robotics Industry Development Plan_, and 2023’s _Robot+ Application Action Plan_ — industrial policy, manufacturing muscle, and patient (state-directed) capital.

That initial 2015 doc sent shudders through Western capitals for reasons Western publics have now learned the hard way: Beijing was pledging to wean itself off strategic Western tech, with devastating consequences for Western industrial bases — when China starts buying less but selling more, millions abroad lose their jobs.

But **second**, on the **why** : collectively, those policy documents really frame robotics as a solution right across China's...

 * **Demographics** — fewer workers, higher wages, and more elderly

 * **Economics** — own the world’s next big strategic export industry, and

 * **Geopolitics** — curbing and then flipping China's reliance on Western tech.




The**intriguing exception** is a fourth objective which those public docs never quite spell out: military uses. The same year Beijing launched _Made in China_ (2015), Xi elevated the separate Military-Civil Fusion strategy that was already calling dibs on any dual-use tech for the People's Liberation Army — or to put it another way, the same viral _Looney Tunes_ robot that just hilariously careened off the track at Beijing’s Ice Ribbon is probably also pretty good at carrying ammo — and eventually, taking shots.

We're not speculating here, btw — we already saw China's new ‘robot wolves’ at the last Victory Day Parade.

And none of this is to diminish the genuine engineering excellence now on display in Beijing either, often by folks more interested in pushing the frontier of human knowledge than any particular East-West score-settling.

Nor is any of this intended to help authoritarian propagandists instil a global sense of awe and inevitability around China’s next big push for industrial dominance.

Rather, it’s simply to note that **none of this is accidental**. And the free world — for all its own impressive (if quiet) robotic advances — now really has some catching up to do, with strategic implications that won’t stay confined to factory floors.
