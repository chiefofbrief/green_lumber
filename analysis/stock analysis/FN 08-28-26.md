# Fabrinet (FN) — Synthesis

**Growth.** Sales growth has accelerated for three straight fiscal years — TTM revenue growth moved from +9.0% (FY24) to +18.6% (FY25) to +35.7% (FY26), and every quarter of FY26 grew faster YoY than the one before it (+21.6% → +35.9% → +39.3% → +44.6%). It's overwhelmingly an AI-data-center story: Data Center revenue is now the largest category (51% of Q4 revenue, +68% YoY), led by DCI (~$1B annualized run rate) and an HPC ramp with a major hyperscaler, with Communications Infrastructure a genuinely broad-based secondary contributor (+40% YoY) and Automotive/Industrial/Other lagging (+8% YoY). Growth is organic, not acquired, and management guided Q1 FY27 to +43% YoY at the midpoint while calling further acceleration "not beyond the bounds of possibility." The clearest near-term ceiling is supply, not demand: component shortages (lasers, memory, certain ASICs) capped what could actually be shipped in FY26. The clearest structural caveat is that this growth still rides on a narrow, if broadening, customer base — the top four customers rose from 45.8% to 57.4% of revenue even as single-name (NVIDIA) risk fell.

**Profitability.** Gross margin is thin and structurally flat — 12.0% TTM, exactly at its 5-year trough, reflecting Fabrinet's contract-manufacturing model (pass-through materials costs) rather than a temporary or reinvestment-driven dip; R&D is negligible and SG&A is shrinking as a share of revenue, so the thin margin isn't buying growth investment. Despite that, operating margin has expanded (9.0% → 10.0% since FY22) on SG&A leverage, and ROIC is strong at 22.4% TTM — the model converts thin per-unit margin into solid capital returns at scale. FCF, however, collapsed to essentially breakeven in FY26 ($4M, a 5-year-low 0.1% of sales) as capex more than doubled (+107.3%) to fund the Building 10 / Navanakorn / Santa Clara capacity buildout while operating cash flow fell on working-capital growth — a deliberate, return-accretive reinvestment choice per management's own framing, not an earnings problem, but it does mean the business is currently generating little discretionary cash.

**Risk.** The four biggest threats, in order: (1) customer concentration — 57.4% of FY26 revenue from four names, with real bargaining-power and demand-durability implications; (2) earnings-quality/cash-conversion drift — OCF/Net Income fell from a 1.39x FY24 peak to 0.54x TTM, a genuine (if explainably growth-driven) signal that reported profit currently overstates cash generation; (3) capital intensity — capex now runs at ~4x D&A and the company took on its first meaningful debt in years (~$75M) shortly after fiscal year-end to help fund it, a modest but real shift from an all-cash posture; and (4) a thin, largely unprotected moat — Fabrinet holds almost no patents and says so itself, relying instead on qualification-driven switching costs and process know-how, with customers' own in-house manufacturing capacity named as its primary competitive threat. Accounting choices reviewed don't point toward earnings inflation — if anything, FY26's discretionary choices (excluding a non-cash gain from non-GAAP results, absorbing a new Pillar Two tax cost rather than offsetting it) skew conservative — and management reads as forthright rather than guarded, though insiders have been net sellers (~$2.93M, zero buys) over the past six months. One unquantified legal contingency (a PRC subsidiary litigation accepted for retrial in July 2026) is disclosed but open-ended.

**Affordability.** The sell-side is broadly bullish (75% Buy, 0% Sell, mean target implying ~64% upside) and valuation isn't extreme by current multiples (EV/Sales 3.18x, GAAP P/E ~32x), but the stock's *trading* has been far more volatile than the fundamentals alone would suggest — a ~246% run from its March 2025 trough to an April 2026 peak, followed by a ~38% pullback (including a ~20-25% single-day drop on a beat-and-raise Q4 print) driven largely by capex/FCF worry and broader AI-hardware risk-off sentiment rather than a reassessment of the growth thesis. The current price sits well above its 24-month trough (+113.6%) but meaningfully below both its own recent peak and the sell-side's current mean target — a partial, not full, re-rating toward caution. Notably, two of our sharper filing-level findings (the structurally thin gross margin and the OCF/Net Income decline) don't appear to be part of the public market narrative at all, which is either an overlooked risk or a sign the market has already implicitly priced a structurally thin-margin, capital-intensive business into a moderate multiple.

## How does the company make money?

**Takeaways**
- Fabrinet is a contract manufacturer, not a product company: OEM customers hand it a design, and it does the process engineering, supply chain management, assembly, packaging, and testing, mostly out of Thailand, earning a manufacturing margin rather than a product margin — it never owns the IP or brand of what it ships (10-K; earnings call).
- The business is now dominated by data-center-bound products. In the most recent quarter (Q4 FY26), "Data Center" became the largest and fastest-growing category — 51% of revenue ($669M, +68% YoY) — ahead of Communications Infrastructure (31%, $413M, +40% YoY) and Automotive/Industrial/Other (18%, $234M, +8% YoY) (earnings call).
- Revenue is concentrated in the handful of customers whose products it builds: Cisco (20%), NVIDIA (16%), Nokia (11%), and Amazon (11%) together made up 57.4% of FY26 revenue (10-K).

**Supporting Data**
Fabrinet calls itself "often the sole outsourced manufacturing partner" its OEM customers use for a given product, and it protects customer IP with a "factory-within-a-factory" model — physically dedicated engineering teams and production lines per customer rather than shared lines (10-K). Revenue is recognized when the finished, customer-specified product ships (10-K). It does not design its own products and holds essentially no product IP of its own (see competitive-advantages answer below).

Fabrinet just changed how it reports revenue, which matters for reading the trend. Through FY26 it reported "Optical Communications" (data center + telecom) vs. "Non-Optical Communications" (HPC, automotive, industrial, medical, sensors); starting Q4 FY26 it recast to three end-market categories — Data Center, Communications Infrastructure, and Automotive/Industrial/Other — based on where the product is ultimately deployed rather than what it technically is (earnings call). Under the new scheme, full FY26 revenue was $2.23B (47.9%) Data Center, $1.55B (33.3%) Communications Infrastructure, and $0.87B (18.8%) Automotive/Industrial/Other (10-K) — but the Q4 quarter alone already shows Data Center at 51%, meaning the mix shifted further toward data center as the year progressed.

Within Data Center, the largest sub-driver is data center interconnect (DCI) modules, which reached "close to a $1 billion" annualized run rate, plus a High Performance Computing (HPC) program with a major hyperscaler and a growing slate of new transceiver programs (earnings call). Within Communications Infrastructure, growth is broad-based across telecom systems, satellite communications, and telecom components, with Nokia (via its Infinera acquisition) newly crossing the 10%-customer threshold in FY26 (earnings call; 10-K).

## What are its competitive advantages?

**Takeaways**
- Fabrinet's edge is not patented technology or brand — it is process-engineering depth and the switching costs created by a lengthy, rigorous customer qualification process (3–6 months or longer), reinforced by capacity scale few peers can match. The company itself says patents "have not played a significant role" in protecting its business (10-K).
- It does essentially no product R&D — R&D spend is a rounding error (numbers.md shows R&D/Sales at 0.0%, and the 10-K's SG&A walk shows only a ~$1.2M R&D increase against $4.6B of revenue) — which makes sense for a manufacturer that builds to customers' designs rather than its own, but also means the "IP" it protects is process know-how and trade secrets, not something it can license or assert. [INFERRED: numbers.md, 10-K — R&D is immaterial and folded into SG&A rather than broken out]
- Capital efficiency looks like a genuine differentiator: 22.4% TTM ROIC, well above the one disclosed peer in the data (LITE at -161.9%, though LITE is a component/product company, not an EMS peer, so this single comparison should be read cautiously) (numbers.md).

**Supporting Data**
On low-cost production: Fabrinet describes its "flexible low-cost manufacturing platform" (Thailand-based, low labor cost) as one reason it can win and keep customers, and thin gross margins (12.0% TTM, essentially flat at its 5-year trough of 12.0%) are typical of the contract-manufacturing industry generally, where materials are pass-through and the majority of cost of revenue (10-K). Thin margin here is a feature of the business model (EMS/contract manufacturing), not obvious evidence of price leadership or its absence — margin is addressed in more depth in the gross-margin question below.

On IP: the company's competitive-strengths section in the 10-K leans on "advanced optical and electro-mechanical process technologies," "strategic alignment with customers," and its "factory-within-a-factory" IP-segregation model, not on patents. It holds "a relatively small number" of PRC patents with expirations through 2040, and states plainly that historically "patents have not played a significant role in the protection of our proprietary rights" (10-K). Its actual moat is procedural: the 3–6 month qualification process required before a customer will place volume orders creates real switching costs and "greater visibility into product life cycles and longer-term customer engagements" once won (10-K).

On distribution/brand: Fabrinet has no consumer-facing brand — its "brand" with OEM customers is reputation for execution. Management frames this as self-reinforcing: "the better we execute for customers, the more business they give us" (earnings call), and cites the Nokia/Infinera relationship — where Fabrinet's standing with the acquired Infinera business helped it win incremental Nokia business — as a concrete example (earnings call).

On R&D effectiveness: the company does not meaningfully invest in its own R&D (R&D/Sales ≈ 0% every year in numbers.md), which is consistent with its role as a manufacturer rather than a designer — customer OEMs, not Fabrinet, own the underlying product innovation. This means R&D intensity is not a lens through which to judge Fabrinet's competitiveness the way it would be for a component maker like Lumentum (LITE); Fabrinet's equivalent investment is capacity and process capability, discussed further below.

## How and why are sales growing?

**Takeaways**
- Growth has accelerated for three straight fiscal years and the pace of that acceleration has itself been building: TTM revenue growth moved from +9.0% (FY24, decelerating -7.9pp vs the year before) to +18.6% (FY25, +9.6pp faster) to +35.7% (FY26, +17.1pp faster still) (numbers.md).
- On a quarterly basis, every quarter of FY26 grew faster YoY than the one before it — +21.6% → +35.9% → +39.3% → +44.6% — though the pace of that acceleration itself bounced around (+0.9pp, then a sharp +14.3pp, cooling to +3.4pp, then reaccelerating to +5.4pp) rather than climbing smoothly (numbers.md).
- The growth is overwhelmingly an AI/data-center story: Data Center revenue grew 68% YoY in Q4 and is now the largest category (51% of revenue), led by DCI (~$1B annualized run rate) and an HPC ramp with a major hyperscaler; Communications Infrastructure (+40% YoY) is a secondary but genuinely broad-based contributor; Automotive/Industrial/Other lags badly (+8% YoY) (earnings call).
- The growth is organic, not acquired — the one investment made this year (a ~14–16% minority stake in Raytec Semiconductor) is not consolidated and doesn't add revenue (10-K; 10-Q) — but it is also concentrated in a handful of customer relationships, which is a caveat that matters for durability (addressed in the customer-concentration question below).

**Supporting Data**
Annually, TTM revenue climbed from $2.88B (FY24) to $3.42B (FY25, +18.6%) to $4.64B (FY26, +35.7%) (numbers.md). Framed by management on the Q4 call: a 10-year stretch through FY24 at 17% CAGR, then 19% growth in FY25, then 36% in FY26 — "12 consecutive quarters of record revenues and six consecutive quarters of accelerating year-over-year growth" (earnings call).

Quarterly YoY growth in FY26 was Q1 +21.6% (accel +0.9pp) → Q2 +35.9% (accel +14.3pp) → Q3 +39.3% (accel +3.4pp) → Q4 +44.6% (accel +5.4pp) (numbers.md). Every quarter beat the year-ago comparison by a wider margin than the prior quarter did — genuine acceleration throughout the year — but the *rate* of that acceleration was uneven, peaking in Q2 before cooling and then picking back up, which matters because it means the trend, while consistently positive, isn't a straight line investors should extrapolate mechanically.

The driver is concentrated in one end market. Data Center revenue was $669M in Q4, +68% YoY and +13% sequentially, now 51% of total revenue and growing faster than the company overall (earnings call). Within it, DCI is "close to a $1 billion" annualized run rate and datacom/HPC continue to ramp with a major hyperscaler, plus newly-won direct-to-hyperscaler and merchant transceiver programs beginning to ship in small qualification quantities (earnings call). Communications Infrastructure grew a healthy 40% YoY on broad-based demand (telecom systems, satellite communications, telecom components), helped by Nokia's acquisition of Infinera, a long-standing Fabrinet customer, which crossed Nokia into 10%-customer territory in FY26 (earnings call; 10-K). Automotive/Industrial/Other, by contrast, grew just 8% YoY, with automotive itself flat-to-declining and EV-charging/LiDAR providing the modest offset (earnings call).

On whether growth is organic: the 10-K states that acquisition-related cost structure "has historically been relatively insignificant" to the business, and the only capital deployed toward an external company this year was a minority (~14–16%), non-controlling equity stake in Raytec Semiconductor to support co-packaged-optics (CPO) capability — accounted for at cost, not consolidated, and explicitly not expected to bring "significant influence" (10-K; 10-Q). A post-FY26 $76.9M purchase of a US manufacturing facility (July 2026) is a capacity addition, not an acquired revenue stream (10-K). The growth is demand- and capacity-driven, not M&A-driven.

One caveat worth flagging here rather than only in the customer-concentration answer: the four 10%-plus customers together went from 45.8% of revenue in FY25 to 57.4% in FY26 (10-K) — the growth, while spread across three end markets, is still riding on a small number of relationships expanding, and NVIDIA specifically *declined* over 20% for the year even as total revenue grew ~36%, meaning the "AI story" here is not simply "NVIDIA demand" but a broader, though still narrow, set of hyperscaler-adjacent programs (earnings call; management did not dispute this framing when an analyst raised it).

## Is there a reasonable expectation of accelerated or continued future sales growth?

**Takeaways**
- Guidance points to continued (if not accelerated) growth: Q1 FY27 revenue guide of $1.375–1.425B implies +43% YoY at the midpoint, and management said on the call that further *acceleration* in FY27 is "not beyond the bounds of possibility" given new programs and the fading NVIDIA headwind — though this is a one-quarter guide, not a firm multi-year commitment (earnings call).
- Management is backing that confidence with capital: capex jumped 107% YoY to $0.25B in FY26 (numbers.md), and the company is executing a multi-year plan to roughly triple its revenue capacity from a $5.3B FY26 exit run-rate to $12.5–14B, via Building 10, the Navanakorn campus, Santa Clara expansion, and land for two more Chonburi factories (earnings call).
- Fabrinet sits inside a sector our own sector notes flag as a "durable" layer of the AI buildout — optical networking, cited there as heading toward a $100B market by 2030 with software-like switching-cost moats — but Fabrinet itself is one step removed from that moat: it assembles designs owned by others, and the specific next-generation opportunity it is most excited about (CPO/NPO) is still early and largely "in front of" the company, not yet a meaningful revenue line (Sectors.md; earnings call).
- The clearest near-term constraint is not demand but supply: component shortages (lasers, memory, certain ASICs) already capped what Fabrinet could ship in FY26 and are expected to persist into FY27 — a real ceiling on how fast reported revenue can grow even though underlying demand is described as exceeding what's shippable (earnings call).

**Supporting Data**
Sector backdrop: our sector notes classify AI-infrastructure hardware as "Mid-Late acceleration" — already decommoditized (innovation currently matters) but with more compute leading to lower marginal returns over time, which eventually favors low-cost producers (Sectors.md). Separately, the notes single out networking/optics as a comparatively durable piece of the AI stack — "Ethernet optics are on a path to a $100 billion market by 2030" — with moats closer to software than to the more cyclical, capacity-driven memory trade (Sectors.md). This is a favorable macro setting for Fabrinet's largest end market, though it is a read on the sector generally, not on Fabrinet's specific share of it.

S-curve positioning: Fabrinet's largest current driver — pluggable transceivers, DCI modules, and HPC subsystems — is already at meaningful scale (DCI alone near a $1B run rate), so that piece looks mid-cycle rather than early. The technology management is most excited about longer-term, co-packaged optics (CPO) and near-packaged optics (NPO), is explicitly early: "we are already seeing some CPO revenue, but the amounts are relatively small at this point," working across three customer programs with timing still undisclosed (earnings call, Q3). Management calls NPO "a more near-term opportunity than CPO," aided by the new Raytec relationship, but neither is a demonstrated revenue driver yet (earnings call, Q4).

Market share: the data does not disclose a market-share figure for Fabrinet in DCI, transceivers, or HPC — management speaks qualitatively ("our position supplying the DCI market is very strong," "we have all of the major players there as customers") but no filing or call gives a percentage. **This is a real gap**: without a share number, it's hard to independently judge how much of the segment's growth Fabrinet is capturing versus how much is the market itself growing (10-K; earnings calls — gap flagged, not filled with an estimate).

Moat durability: the qualification-driven switching costs and factory-within-a-factory IP protection described above are real, but the company's own risk factors name customers' internal manufacturing capacity as its primary competitive threat, citing the 2008–09 downturn as a precedent where customers with idle capacity in-sourced production (10-Q). Given how little of Fabrinet's advantage rests on legally protectable IP (see competitive-advantages answer), the moat is best described as durable so long as demand stays strong enough that customers don't have idle capacity of their own — a real but conditional durability, not an absolute one.

Investment and management commitment: beyond the capex and capacity plan above, management frames capacity decisions as low-risk, high-return: building the 2M-square-foot Building 10 costs ~$130-132M against ~$3.0-3.5B of revenue capacity, with roughly six months of operating profit at full utilization covering the entire build, and a downside case (no new demand materializes) costing only "about 50 basis points" of gross margin headwind (earnings call, Q3). This is a genuinely aggressive, AI-driven capital allocation stance, and management explicitly frames the company as "leveraging" AI-driven demand (CPO, NPO, HPC, hyperscaler-direct programs) rather than merely benefiting passively from it.

Challenges to growth: (1) component supply constraints — lasers, memory, and certain ASICs — already suppressed FY26 datacom shipments below demand and are expected to continue into FY27, meaning reported revenue growth may understate underlying demand growth but is nonetheless capped by it in the near term (earnings call); (2) customer concentration — the top-4-customer share rising to 57.4% of FY26 revenue means any one relationship slowing (as NVIDIA did this year) is a direct drag, even if offset by others; (3) FX — a weakening US dollar against the Thai baht is a persistent margin (and by extension, reinvestment capacity) headwind (10-K); (4) policy uncertainty — a possible US ban on Chinese-made transceivers is framed by management as plausibly *positive* for Fabrinet (it doesn't manufacture for Chinese customers) but "early days" and could also disrupt the broader supply ecosystem Fabrinet depends on (earnings call, Q4).

## Is it highly dependent on a small number of customers?

**Takeaways**
- Yes, heavily: four customers each contributed 10%+ of revenue in FY26 — Cisco (20%), NVIDIA (16%), Nokia (11%), Amazon (11%) — together 57.4% of total revenue, up from 45.8% in FY25 and 48.5% in FY24 (10-K).
- The picture is nuanced, not simply "worse": single-name risk in the largest customer actually fell (NVIDIA went from 27.6% of revenue in FY25 to 16.3% in FY26), but the *combined* top-tier share still rose because two more customers (Nokia, Amazon) crossed the 10% threshold — so Fabrinet is diversifying across more large names, not away from large-customer dependence overall. [INFERRED: 10-K figures — management's "diversifying" framing on the Q4 call is true at the single-customer level but the aggregate concentration trend still points up]
- Accounts receivable are similarly concentrated (three customers ≥10% of AR as of both June 2025 and March 2026), so a payment problem at any one large customer would hit the balance sheet, not just future revenue (10-Q; 10-K).

**Supporting Data**
FY26 disclosure: "During fiscal years 2026 and 2025, we had four and two customers, respectively, that each contributed 10% or more of our revenues... During fiscal year 2026, Cisco Systems, Inc., NVIDIA Corporation, Nokia Corporation and Amazon.com, Inc. contributed 19.9%, 16.3%, 10.7% and 10.5%, respectively" (10-K). The nine-month (Q3 FY26 YTD) view shows the same trend: four customers at 58.1% of revenue, versus two customers at 47.5% in the same period a year earlier (10-Q).

The company is explicit that this is structural, not incidental: "The optical communications market we serve is highly concentrated. Therefore, we expect a significant percentage of our revenues will continue to come from a small number of customers" (10-K). It also flags the compounding effect of industry consolidation — Nokia/Infinera, Lumentum/NeoPhotonics, Coherent/II-VI, and Cisco/Acacia are all cited as past customer-side mergers that reduce the number of potential customers Fabrinet can sell to, in some cases because the combined company gained in-house capacity (10-K).

The risk is not just top-line volatility. Concentrated customers carry "substantial purchasing power and leverage in negotiating contracts," and Fabrinet notes that master supply agreements guarantee no minimum volume — business is awarded project-by-project, and "some of our customers have at times significantly reduced or delayed" orders (10-K). Combined with three customers making up 10%+ of accounts receivable, a financial problem at any one of the four could hit both future revenue and cash already earned (10-Q).

## Does it have a worthwhile gross margin? If not, is there a good reason why the margin is low?

**Takeaways**
- Gross margin is thin and essentially flat at its 5-year low: 12.0% TTM, exactly matching the 5-year trough of 12.0%, and slowly eroding since FY23's 12.7% peak in the disclosed window even as revenue nearly tripled (numbers.md).
- The low margin is a structural feature of Fabrinet's contract-manufacturing business model — materials are the majority of cost of revenue and are largely passed through — not evidence of unsustainable pricing or a temporary problem; it is *not* mainly a reinvestment story either, since R&D is negligible and SG&A is shrinking as a share of sales even as gross margin holds flat.
- The only available peer comparison in the data (LITE at 41.7% gross margin) is not a fair read on Fabrinet's margin, because LITE designs and owns its own photonics products (11.8% R&D/Sales) while Fabrinet manufactures to customer designs and does essentially no product R&D — comparing their gross margins conflates two different business models. **This is a real gap**: the data doesn't include a true EMS/contract-manufacturing peer (e.g., Celestica, Jabil, Sanmina — all named as competitors in the 10-K) to benchmark Fabrinet's margin against companies with a comparable model.
- Despite flat-to-thin gross margin, operating margin has been expanding (9.0% FY22 → 10.0% FY26, TTM) purely on SG&A leverage (2.0% of sales, down from 3.3%), and ROIC is strong at 22.4% TTM — so the margin structure, while thin per unit, is translating into healthy overall capital returns.

**Supporting Data**
Five-year gross margin history: 12.3% (FY22) → 12.7% (FY23) → 12.4% (FY24) → 12.1% (FY25) → 12.0% (FY26 TTM), against a 5-year trough of 12.0% — meaning the current margin sits at the low end of its own recent range even after several years of dramatic revenue growth (numbers.md). Quarterly margin in FY26 bounced in a narrow band — 11.9% (Q1) → 12.2% (Q2) → 11.6% (Q3) → 12.3% (Q4) — noisy but directionally flat, not clearly improving or worsening within the year (numbers.md).

Why the margin is low: cost of revenues is dominated by materials, most of which are "customized for [customer] products and often sourced from a single supplier," with margins tied to fixed per-quarter pricing contingent on assumed production yields (10-K) — a classic pass-through-heavy contract-manufacturing structure rather than a product margin. Management's own explanation for near-term softness leans on two temporary factors, not structural reinvestment: FX headwinds from the Thai baht (expected to persist at a similar level quarter to quarter) and short-term inefficiencies from ramping "a large number of new programs across multiple growth vectors simultaneously," which management expects to fade "as these programs mature" (earnings call, Q3). This is a different story from a company deliberately sacrificing margin to fund R&D or sales investment — R&D/Sales is ~0% and SG&A/Sales has been *falling*, so the margin pressure is coming from FX and ramp costs, not deliberate reinvestment (numbers.md).

Guidance: management guided Q4 gross margin to be "similar to Q3" and flagged "usual first-quarter expense seasonality" as a temporary Q1 FY27 headwind, while still projecting "continued operating leverage as revenue grows" at the operating-margin level (earnings call, Q3 & Q4). Management separately quantified the downside risk from the ongoing capacity buildout as minor — roughly 50 basis points of gross margin drag in a scenario where new factory space goes unused — implying the buildout itself isn't expected to structurally depress margin (earnings call, Q3).

Is it "worthwhile"? On gross margin alone, no — 12% is thin by most standards, and the one peer in the data (LITE, 41.7%) dwarfs it, though that comparison is apples-to-oranges given the different business models (numbers.md). But operating margin has expanded five points since FY22 relative to gross margin's slight decline (9.0% → 10.0% vs. 12.3% → 12.0%), driven entirely by SG&A discipline, and ROIC of 22.4% TTM (vs. a 5-year average of 20.5%) shows the business converts its thin per-unit margin into strong returns on invested capital at scale (numbers.md) — the margin should be read alongside these capital-efficiency metrics, not in isolation.

## Is it generating FCF? If not, is there a good reason why?

**Takeaways**
- FCF collapsed to essentially breakeven in FY26 — $4 million for the full year (0.1% of sales), the exact 5-year low, down from $0.21B (6.0% of sales) in FY25 and a FY24 peak of $0.37B (12.7% of sales) (numbers.md; earnings call).
- This is a deliberate reinvestment story, not a profitability problem: FY26 operating cash flow actually *fell* 21.8% YoY (to $257M) even as net income rose, because inventory and receivables grew to support the ramp — and capex more than doubled (+107.3% YoY to $250M) to fund the Building 10 / Navanakorn / Santa Clara capacity buildout, so FCF was squeezed from both directions simultaneously (10-K; numbers.md).
- Individual quarters ran outright negative — free cash outflows of $11M in Q3 FY26 and $37M in Q4 FY26, both explicitly tied by the CFO to capex tied to Building 10 construction and new-site purchases, not to weaker underlying operations (earnings call, Q3 & Q4).
- As with gross margin, the only peer in the data (LITE, 18.3% FCF/Sales TTM) isn't a like-for-like comparison given the different business models — flagged again as a gap rather than used as a real benchmark.

**Supporting Data**
Full-year FCF history: $0.03B (FY22, 1.5% of sales) → $0.15B (FY23, 5.7%) → $0.37B (FY24, 12.7%, the peak) → $0.21B (FY25, 6.0%) → ~$0.004B (FY26, 0.1%, the 5-year trough) (numbers.md). The CFO confirmed the full-year figures directly: "operating cash flow was $257 million and free cash flow was $4 million" for fiscal 2026 (earnings call, Q4).

The cause is capital allocation, not weaker earnings: cost of revenues, gross profit, and operating income all grew in FY26 (operating income +42.7% to $462.9M) (10-K), while OCF fell because "an increase in inventories to support higher demand in the next quarter, and an increase in trade receivables due to timing of collection" outweighed the benefit of higher net income and rising payables (10-K). At the same time, capex jumped from $0.12B to $0.25B (+107.3%) to fund "continued accelerated construction of Building 10, as well as capacity expansions to support the rapid growth across the business" (earnings call, Q3) — CapEx/D&A reached 396.7% TTM, nearly 4x depreciation, consistent with an active expansion phase rather than steady-state maintenance spend (numbers.md).

Quarter by quarter, the CFO was explicit about the drag: "capital expenditure spending of $64 million reflects continued accelerated construction of Building 10... As a result, free cash flow was an outflow of $11 million in the quarter" (Q3), and "Capital expenditures increased to $92 million with ongoing construction of Building 10... Free cash flow was an outflow of $37 million in the quarter" (Q4) (earnings call). [Note: the numbers.md quarterly table shows somewhat smaller capex figures for these quarters than the amounts the CFO cited on the calls — the annual totals reconcile cleanly between the two sources, but the intra-year quarterly split should be read with that discrepancy in mind.]

Is there a good reason for the weak FCF? Yes — it is capacity investment ahead of demand, which management frames as return-accretive rather than cautionary: a 2-million-square-foot factory costs roughly $130-132M to build against $3.0-3.5B of eventual revenue capacity, with "about six months' worth of operating profit" at full utilization covering the entire build cost (earnings call, Q3). Funding has come from the balance sheet ($875M+ cash, no debt through fiscal year-end) rather than external financing, though the company did draw a new ~$75M term loan shortly after fiscal year-end explicitly to "support the Company's capital expenditures" (10-K) — a sign that the pace of investment is beginning to exceed what cash on hand alone comfortably funds.

## Are liabilities and expenses reasonable and manageable?

**Takeaways**
- Debt is negligible: effectively zero through fiscal year-end ($0.00B TTM, 0.1% Debt/Sales, "no outstanding debt" per the 10-K), against $875M+ of cash — this is not a company at any near-term risk of being unable to service debt in a downturn (10-K; numbers.md).
- Shortly after fiscal year-end, Fabrinet took on its first meaningful debt in years — a ~$75M term loan explicitly to help fund capex — small relative to cash and revenue, but a real shift from an all-cash capacity-funding posture, worth watching if the pace of capex continues (10-K).
- The dominant, fast-growing expense is capex, not SBC or S&M: capex more than doubled YoY (+107.3% to $250M) and is running at ~4x D&A (396.7% TTM), while SBC (0.7% of sales) and SG&A (2.0% of sales, shrinking) both remain modest and well controlled (numbers.md).
- A dedicated sales & marketing figure isn't disclosed — Fabrinet reports a combined SG&A line — so S&M intensity specifically can't be assessed directly; SG&A/Sales trending down is the best available proxy, and it points to genuine operating leverage, not expense creep. [gap flagged]

**Supporting Data**
Debt and covenants: the 10-K states plainly that as of June 26, 2026, the company had "cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments of $875.1 million... and no outstanding debt." Existing credit facilities (2019 and 2023 agreements with Bank of Ayudhya) require Fabrinet Thailand to maintain debt-to-equity ≤1.5x and the parent company ≤1.0x, and the company states it "was in compliance with all of their financial covenants" as of both the 10-Q and 10-K dates (10-Q; 10-K). Maturities are decades out (2039, 2043, 2044), so there is no near-term refinancing wall (10-K). On August 17, 2026 — after fiscal year-end — the company amended its 2019 facility and drew a new $75.0 million term loan (Thai baht 2.50 billion), with proceeds "used to support the Company's capital expenditures" (10-K); interest coverage has historically been enormous (6,609.5x TTM) simply because interest expense has been near zero, so even with this new debt, coverage capacity is not a concern (numbers.md).

CapEx and forward commitments: capex reached $250M in FY26 (+107.3% YoY), and the company additionally carries $108.1M of capital-expenditure commitments to third parties and a much larger $3.15 billion of total purchase obligations (mostly inventory-related) as of fiscal year-end, up from $2.74 billion nine months earlier (10-K; 10-Q). These purchase obligations are contractually flexible — "open purchase orders... generally give the Company the option to cancel, reschedule and/or adjust its requirements" before delivery (10-Q) — so they represent a real but not rigid forward liability.

SBC: modest and declining as a share of revenue — 1.2% (FY22) down to 0.7% (FY26 TTM) — with FY26 dollar amounts of $11.5M in cost of revenues and $23.2M in SG&A (10-K; numbers.md). Treated as a cash expense, this would trim FY26 operating income by roughly $35M against $462.9M of reported operating income — a modest, not distorting, adjustment.

S&M: not broken out separately; Fabrinet discloses a single SG&A line that fell from 3.3% of revenue (FY22) to 2.0% (FY26 TTM), which the company itself frames as intentional operating leverage rather than under-investment — "our growth model does not require significant incremental operating expense" (earnings call, Q4). Without a standalone S&M figure, this trend is the best proxy available, and it points to expense discipline rather than a hidden growing liability.

## Do accounting choices appear to be inflating or depressing reported earnings?

**Takeaways**
- The master check (OCF / Net Income) shows real deterioration — from a FY24 peak of 1.39x down to 0.54x TTM — meaning reported net income currently overstates the cash the business is generating; the 10-K attributes this specifically to inventory and receivables growth funding the ramp, which is a normal byproduct of rapid scaling rather than a sign of manufactured earnings, but it is worth monitoring if it doesn't recover as growth normalizes (numbers.md; 10-K).
- Nothing else in the checklist points toward earnings inflation — restructuring charges are small and shrinking, SBC is modest, goodwill is zero, there's no serial M&A, and cash-flow presentation matches the plain operational explanation given in the 10-K.
- If anything, two specific FY26 items argue for *conservative* rather than aggressive accounting: management excluded a $56.7M non-cash *gain* from non-GAAP earnings (lowering the adjusted number), and absorbed a real, new $57.4M Pillar Two tax cost rather than finding an offsetting benefit — the effective tax rate more than doubled YoY (6.4% → 14.8%) (10-K).
- One disclosed, unquantified risk worth carrying forward: a PRC lawsuit against a Fabrinet subsidiary (Casix) was accepted for retrial by the PRC Supreme Court in July 2026; the company states it cannot yet estimate potential liability (10-K).

**Supporting Data**
Earnings vs. cash: OCF/Net Income ran 0.62x (FY22) → 0.86x (FY23) → 1.39x (FY24) → 0.99x (FY25) → 0.54x (FY26 TTM) (numbers.md) — a clear multi-year decline, and FY26's ratio means barely half of reported net income showed up as operating cash. The 10-K's own explanation is consistent with organic scaling rather than manipulation: OCF fell YoY specifically because of "an increase in inventories to support higher demand in the next quarter, and an increase in trade receivables due to timing of collection," partially offset by rising payables and higher net income (10-K). This is the most important single accounting-quality flag in the data and should temper how much weight later valuation work puts on GAAP net income alone.

Revenue quality: three customers make up 10%+ of accounts receivable, mirroring revenue concentration (10-Q; 10-K) — a credit-risk flag, not a revenue-recognition red flag. The 10-K discloses a customer-storage arrangement resembling bill-and-hold, but revenue there is recognized only once goods are complete and transferred to Fabrinet's own warehouse at the customer's written request — a standard, disclosed practice rather than a device to pull revenue forward (10-K). No related-party revenue or round-tripping is disclosed anywhere in the data.

Reserves/timing: restructuring costs were small and fell further — $1.4M (FY25) to $0.1M (FY26) (10-K) — not a lever being used to manage earnings across periods.

Capitalization/depreciation: CapEx is running at 396.7% of D&A TTM (roughly 4x), which is expected during an active capacity build-out and not, on its own, evidence of aggressive capitalization versus peers — but it does mean D&A will step up mechanically in future years as Building 10 and other new sites come online, a real future cost to watch against margin guidance (numbers.md).

Non-GAAP and SBC: SBC itself is small (0.7% of sales) so isn't a major lever in adjusted results. The Q4 non-GAAP walk excluded a $56.7 million non-cash gain from remeasuring the Raytec investment (a *conservative* exclusion — it lowers non-GAAP earnings relative to GAAP) and a $57.4 million Pillar Two tax provision (a cost exclusion, which raises non-GAAP earnings) — one adjustment cuts, the other adds, arguing against a pattern of one-directional flattery; net non-GAAP EPS ($4.10) still landed above GAAP EPS ($3.83) for the quarter because the tax add-back was larger (10-K; earnings call).

Tax: the effective tax rate rose sharply, from 6.4% (FY25) to 14.8% (FY26), because of the new OECD Pillar Two top-up tax regime — the company recognized $57.4 million of current tax expense related to it (10-K). This is the opposite of a company leaning on a falling tax rate to flatter EPS; FY26 GAAP earnings absorbed a genuine new tax headwind.

Acquisitions and goodwill: Goodwill/Sales is 0.0% throughout the disclosed five-year history (numbers.md) — no acquisition-driven asset inflation or big-bath write-off risk on the balance sheet. The one investment made in FY26, the ~14-16% minority stake in Raytec, is accounted for at cost and not consolidated (10-K; 10-Q), consistent with the organic-growth read from the sales-growth question above.

Off-balance-sheet and governance: no debt of consequence through fiscal year-end (the post-FYE $75M term loan is covered under liabilities above), no pension disclosed, and purchase obligations ($3.15B) are the main forward commitment, described as generally cancelable pre-delivery (10-Q). The filings report explicitly notes no auditor changes or other governance flags ("None") (FN_filings_report.md). The one disclosed legal contingency — the PRC Casix unjust-enrichment claim, accepted for retrial by the PRC Supreme Court on July 30, 2026 — is disclosed with the company stating it "cannot quantify any potential liability... because of the early stage of this litigation" (10-K); this is a real, open-ended risk to carry forward, not an accounting-quality issue per se.

## Is management trustworthy?

**Takeaways**
- Management reads as forthright rather than guarded on operating results — they attributed FY26's datacom shortfall directly to supply constraints rather than soft demand ("we could have shipped a lot more if we had those components"), and proactively explained a revenue-category change with a full reconciliation rather than obscuring it (earnings call, Q3 & Q4).
- Tone became more confident, not more defensive, from the Q3 call to the Q4 call — specificity increased (12 consecutive quarters of record revenue, explicit capacity run-rate math) rather than softening into vaguer language, which is more consistent with genuine execution than a story being carefully managed around a weak spot.
- Insiders were net sellers over the past six months (~$2.93M sold, zero bought, across two directors), and a separate news item flags an August 2026 CEO stock sale (~$1.7M, ~7% of his prior direct holdings) — a mildly negative signal on its own, but the amounts are small relative to each insider's remaining stake and the company's $15B+ market cap, and read as routine diversification rather than a coordinated exit (management.md; FN_data_summary.md; news.md).
- The accounting-quality review above found no evidence of earnings manipulation, and two specific choices (excluding a non-cash gain from non-GAAP results; absorbing a new tax cost rather than offsetting it) point toward disclosure-forward rather than aggressive accounting — a positive complement to the qualitative read on management.

**Supporting Data**
Transparency: on the Q3 call, management didn't soften the datacom miss — "we could have shipped a lot more if we had those components... Without these supply constraints, datacom revenue would have been a new record by a wide margin," and named the specific commodities involved (lasers, memory, certain ASICs) rather than speaking generically about "supply chain issues" (earnings call, Q3). Tone shift: in Q3, management described itself as "not unduly concerned long term" about supply constraints while still flagging near-term shipping impact; by Q4, the framing had shifted to explicit optimism — "we are entering fiscal '27 better positioned than at any other point in our history," with management calling further FY27 acceleration "not beyond the bounds of possibility" (earnings call, Q3 & Q4). How accounting reflects on management: as detailed in the prior question, FY26's accounting choices skew conservative where they had discretion — excluding a non-cash *gain* from non-GAAP results (which lowers the adjusted number) and absorbing a new, real Pillar Two tax cost rather than finding an offset (effective tax rate roughly doubled) — and the one open legal contingency (the PRC Casix litigation) is disclosed with an explicit statement that liability can't yet be quantified, rather than downplayed (10-K). Combined with candid handling of the supply-constraint shortfall, the overall picture is a management team that reports plainly rather than manages the narrative, tempered only by ordinary customer-specific discretion and the mild caution warranted by six months of insider selling with no offsetting buying.
