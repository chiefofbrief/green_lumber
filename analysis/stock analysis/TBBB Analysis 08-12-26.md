# BBB Foods (TBBB / Tiendas 3B) — Pre-Q2 FY2026 Breakdown

*As of 2026-08-12, on the eve of Q2 2026 earnings. The company reports in Mexican pesos (MXN) under IFRS; roughly 17.8 pesos per US dollar. Operating figures below are in pesos; price and market cap are in USD.*

## Synthesis and Takeaways

- **The cash engine is real and high-quality; the GAAP losses are an accounting illusion, not a broken business.** Operating cash flow is about MXN 5.5B trailing, growing at a 34% annual clip in near-perfect lockstep with revenue, at a 6.6% cash margin (FMP; 20-F FY2025; 6-K Q1 2026). The reported net losses come almost entirely from non-cash stock compensation and IFRS-16 lease interest — not from operations. In Q1 2026 the MXN 558M net loss reverses to roughly MXN 160M of profit once you add back the MXN 722M of stock comp (6-K Q1 2026).
- **Growth is exceptional and, unusually, driven by volume rather than price.** Revenue rose 33.5% in Q1 2026 and same-store sales grew 16%, and management confirms that growth is about two-thirds more transactions and bigger baskets, one-third price and mix — the healthiest kind of retail growth (6-K Q1 2026; Q1 2026 call). The engine is roughly 580 net new stores a year into an under-served market.
- **The single most important number is the stock-comp cliff in Q3 2026.** The Liquidity Event Share Plan that creates almost all of the GAAP loss finishes vesting after Q3 2026, which sets up the first GAAP profit since the 2024 IPO sometime in 2027 (20-F notes). That is a genuine re-rating catalyst because it opens the stock to funds that cannot hold money-losers. The honest caveat: management said plainly it will keep and even expand equity comp, so the losses shrink toward roughly 1–2% of revenue, they do not vanish (Q4 2025 call).
- **Free cash flow is thin and, counting stock comp honestly, owner-earnings are negative today.** Capex is growing about twice as fast as revenue — guided to MXN 5.2B for 2026, up 56% — which squeezes free cash flow to about MXN 1.7B trailing. Subtract the MXN 2.5B of stock comp and owner-earnings are about **negative MXN 800M** (FMP; Q1 2026 call). The company is deliberately consuming more than it generates to fund store growth.
- **The stock is priced for continuation, not perfection — but it is near all-time highs, not a beaten-down bargain.** It trades around 1x revenue, about 18x trailing and 15x forward adjusted EBITDA, at a 6.3% cash yield — reasonable for 30%+ growth, but the shares have nearly doubled off their September 2025 low and sit at 89% of the 52-week range (market data; FMP). This is a compounder you are paying up for, not a distressed name.
- **The whole debate compresses into the imminent Q2 print.** Q2 2026 (call announced July 17, due in August) is the first real test of whether Q4 2025's alarming 0.6% same-store-sales quarter was a one-off — it snapped back to 16% in Q1 — or the first crack of saturation in core Central Mexico. Same-store sales below about 10% would validate the bear case (Q4 2025 call; 6-K Q1 2026).

Put together, this looks like paying about **75 to 80 cents for a dollar of value**. The operator is elite, the cash is real, the losses are finite and dated to a known endpoint, and the shareholder register is high-quality (a top-5 Druckenmiller position, JP Morgan Overweight, an HSBC upgrade). But owner-earnings are negative right now, capex is aggressive, and the growth rate is clearly decelerating — so the discount is real but it is not a slam-dunk bargain until the next print settles the timing-versus-saturation question.

## Growth

The headline is strong in level, softening in trend, and led entirely by units and volume.

TBBB runs the hard-discount grocery playbook — small stores, ~58% private label, minimal marketing, low prices for low- and middle-income Mexican households — the same model BIM used to compound for decades in Turkey. Management is explicit about the comparison, calling private-label expansion "like a time machine" (Q1 2026 call). Revenue reached about MXN 83.3B trailing, and FY2025 revenue was MXN 78.2B, up 36% (20-F FY2025). (Note: the FMP feed shows FY2025 at MXN 73.3B — a known data-sourcing gap for this foreign filer; the 20-F figure is the real one.)

The growth is unusually clean. In Q1 2026, revenue rose 33.5%, transactions rose 18%, and average ticket rose 13.6% on more items per basket rather than price — with same-store sales of 16% (6-K Q1 2026). Management breaks same-store growth into roughly two-thirds volume, one-third price/mix, and notes there are no receivables and no channel-stuffing risk because essentially all sales are cash at the register (20-F; Q1 2026 call).

The trend, though, is deceleration — expected as the base grows. Annual growth has stepped down from about 39% (2023) to about 30% (2024), and management guides 2026 revenue growth to 29–32% with same-store sales of 13–16%, having historically beaten its own guidance (Q4 2025 call). The one scare was Q4 2025, when same-store sales collapsed to 0.6% versus an 18.3% full year; management attributed it to a record 184 store openings plus 4 distribution centers diluting the base, and could not fully explain the full-year strength either (Q4 2025 call). Q1 2026's rebound to 16% strongly suggests it was a timing effect, but Q2 is the confirmation. On geography — the pre-analysis worry — management says new-store performance is "very consistent across the board, irrespective of geography," which lowers the concentration risk (Q1 2026 call).

## Profitability and Cash

This is the strongest and least disputed part of the story — once you look past GAAP.

- **The reported margin is misleading; the underlying margin is stable and healthy.** GAAP operating margin was -0.7% in FY2025 and -1.0% trailing, but adjusted EBITDA margin has held remarkably steady through four years of aggressive expansion: 5.1% (FY2023), 5.9% (FY2024), 5.6% (FY2025), 5.6% (Q1 2026) (management reconciliation, 6-K/20-F). The entire gap is finite, quantified stock comp.
- **Cash generation is genuine and accelerating.** Operating cash flow was about MXN 5.5B trailing and grew 64% year-over-year in Q1 2026 to MXN 1.96B — faster than revenue, implying improving cash conversion (FMP; 6-K Q1 2026). Over five years OCF compounded at 34%, tracking revenue almost exactly, which is the signature of real earnings rather than accounting flattery.
- **Negative working capital is a feature, not a warning.** Working capital sits around -MXN 6.4B and keeps getting more negative as stores open, because suppliers are paid on 30–90 day terms while customers pay in cash — genuine supplier float, the same advantage Walmart, Aldi, and Lidl ran at this stage (20-F notes).
- **Financial leverage is negligible.** Ex-leases, debt is under 0.5x OCF. The scary-looking headline debt is almost all IFRS-16 lease liability (about MXN 11.7B), an operating obligation funded by store cash flows, not financial risk (20-F notes).
- **Store-level economics are excellent.** Management quantifies roughly MXN 5.5M of capex per store, a 26-month payback, and about 55% cash-on-cash return by year three — implying store-level returns on capital in the 20–30%+ range (Q4 2025 call).

The honest caveat mirrors the AppLovin-style discipline: free cash flow is thin (about MXN 1.7B trailing) because capex is growing about twice as fast as revenue, guided up 56% to MXN 5.2B for 2026. Once stock comp is treated as the real cost it is, owner-earnings are negative (about -MXN 800M). Aggregate return on invested capital is -7.8% trailing (distorted by the non-cash losses); adjusted for stock comp it is a modest but positive ~6–7% at the corporate level, still below mature warehouse-club peer PriceSmart's steady 11.2% (FMP). The margins are also young — clean hard-discount economics only really date from the post-IPO period.

## Risk

The balance sheet is not the risk. The risks are decelerating same-store sales against a fixed cost base, aggressive capex, permanent dilution, and single-country concentration.

- **Same-store sales below ~10% is the thesis-breaker.** The store base carries about MXN 24.4B of undiscounted future lease obligations that do not shrink if sales slow. If same-store growth drops below 10% for two quarters, operating deleverage bites hard and the multiple should compress toward ~12x adjusted EBITDA — roughly $27–28 per share, about 35% downside back toward the 52-week low (20-F notes; thesis scenario math).
- **Capex per store could escalate.** The FRESH (fruit-and-vegetable) rollout and standalone, refrigerated store formats could push capex per store above MXN 7M. Retrofitting refrigeration across 3,000+ existing stores could force another equity raise on top of the existing dilution (Q4 2025 call).
- **Stock comp is a permanent cost, only partly rolling off.** The Liquidity Event plan clears after Q3 2026, but management intends to "continue and even expand" equity comp, so owner-earnings improve gradually rather than snapping positive (Q4 2025 call).
- **It is one country, one segment.** All revenue is Mexican grocery retail, historically concentrated in Central Mexico. New regions look consistent so far, but there is no geographic or business-line diversification if Mexico weakens.
- **Recent dilution with no disclosed use of proceeds.** A follow-on equity offering priced and closed in late May/early June 2026; neither earnings call explained where the money went (Q1 2026 call).
- **Small litigation and a quiet tell.** A payment-terminal receivable was written off for about MXN 230M (~US$13M), with litigation ongoing; the deferred-tax treatment suggests management does not expect to recover it (6-K; 20-F notes). Immaterial in size, but a flag on vendor controls.
- **Thin coverage and no retail base.** Only 6 analysts cover it and there is essentially no social/retail footprint — this is a purely institutional story (Druckenmiller, JP Morgan, HSBC, Goldman). That cuts both ways: little naive-money froth, but also no retail demand left to absorb, and management's own CFO called gross-margin expansion "volatile," so the Q1 +20bps should not be over-extrapolated (Q1 2026 call).

## Narrative

TBBB is trying to become the BIM of Mexico, and the market broadly believes it — the argument is almost entirely about the growth rate, not the model. The company IPO'd in February 2024 and has since compounded revenue in the low-to-mid 30s percent by opening roughly 580 hard-discount stores a year into a large, fragmented, under-penetrated grocery market. The economics are genuinely good: cash sales, no receivables, ~58% private label, negative working capital that funds the business, and store-level returns management pegs at ~55% cash-on-cash by year three (20-F; Q4 2025 call). Where most "adjusted" stories hide a worse GAAP number, here it is the reverse — the GAAP losses overstate the problem, because they are almost all non-cash stock comp from a post-IPO plan plus IFRS-16 lease interest.

The tension is that TBBB is spending aggressively to build the future, so the present looks worse than the business is. Capex is guided up 56% this year, free cash flow is thin, and once you charge stock comp honestly, owner-earnings are negative. The bull case is a dated, mechanical fix: the Liquidity Event Share Plan finishes vesting after Q3 2026, which should surface the first GAAP profit since the IPO in 2027 and let a broader set of institutions own the name. The bear case is that same-store sales — already guided down to 13–16% and briefly frightening at 0.6% in Q4 2025 — keep decelerating toward saturation, at which point a fixed and growing lease base turns operating leverage into operating drag.

Two overhangs recently cleared. The dilutive follow-on offering priced and closed in late May, and the July/August lockup expiration — the question that opened the last earnings call — passed on August 6 with the stock still near its highs, suggesting no wall of insider selling. What remains is a single, near-binary event. The Q2 2026 report, due in August, either confirms that Q4's stumble was a store-opening timing artifact — with same-store sales holding in the mid-teens and the cash engine still compounding — or it shows a second soft quarter and turns the deceleration into a story about a maturing core market. Given elite cash economics and a finite, dated loss on one side, and negative owner-earnings, heavy capex, and single-country concentration on the other, that print is the near-term swing factor for the thesis.
