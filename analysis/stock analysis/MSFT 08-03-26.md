# MSFT Q4 Analysis

*Fiscal Q4 2026 (quarter/year ended June 30, 2026); earnings call July 29, 2026.*
*Sources: official earnings call transcript + FY26 Form 10-K (MD&A and Notes). Figures are as-disclosed; items marked "(my math)" are my arithmetic.*

---

## Question 1 — How diversified are cloud sales outside of frontier models?

**Bottom line:** Microsoft's *recognized* cloud revenue is broadly diversified — about 90% comes from customers that are **not** frontier-model labs. The concentration risk lives in the *contracted backlog*, where one customer — **OpenAI** — looms large. **Anthropic is not a cloud dependency.**

- Of the cloud sales Microsoft actually booked this year (more than $214 billion), "nearly 90%" came from ordinary customers rather than the big AI labs. That leaves only about 10% — roughly $21 billion (my math) — tied to frontier-model companies at all `[Call L146]`.
- If you look at new deals signed this quarter, OpenAI isn't the story. New commercial contracts (Microsoft calls these "bookings") were up 18% when you leave OpenAI out, but only up 10% when you include it. Including OpenAI actually made the growth number *smaller* — because a year ago there was a giant OpenAI deal in the comparison base that's hard to top. So this quarter OpenAI was a drag on the comparison, not a driver `[Call L143]`.
- The one place OpenAI dominates is the backlog — the pile of signed-but-not-yet-delivered contracts, which Microsoft calls "remaining performance obligation" (RPO). **That backlog grew 84% to $678 billion *with* OpenAI included, but only 25% *without* it**. The catch is timing: that huge OpenAI commitment was signed earlier in the year, not this quarter. Microsoft says *all* of this quarter's new backlog came from non-frontier customers, and it warned that the old OpenAI contracts will make the growth rates "lumpy" from quarter to quarter `[Call L144]` `[Call L178]`.
- Why a big backlog doesn't mean big revenue yet: that backlog turns into actual sales slowly — on average over about 2.3 years, with only ~30% expected in the next 12 months. So OpenAI's commitment sits mostly in the "future" bucket, which is why it's huge in the backlog but still only ~10% of the sales Microsoft has actually recognized `[Call L145]`.
- How big is OpenAI as a customer? The 10-K spells it out: Microsoft owns about 25% of OpenAI and recorded **$24.1 billion of revenue from OpenAI** in FY26, with $6.0 billion still owed to it. That's about 7% of Microsoft's total revenue (my math). One important caveat: that $24.1B figure *mixes* OpenAI's Azure usage together with payments the two companies share, so it is not a clean "how much cloud did OpenAI buy" number `[Notes L2005–2017]`.
- Microsoft itself admits the risk in plain terms: these AI partners "are significant customers of Azure," and their spending "may not materialize, may be delayed or reduced, or may decline over time" `[MD&A L284]`.
- Anthropic is not a cloud dependency — it's a company Microsoft *invested in* (which produced a one-time gain, covered in Question 3), and its models are simply offered for sale on Microsoft's Foundry platform alongside everyone else's `[Call L36]` `[Call L38]`.

**Net:** Today's cloud revenue is well spread out (~90% non-frontier). The real OpenAI dependence shows up in the future, contracted book — that's the thing to watch.

## Question 2 — ROI on AI spend, adoption/usage, AI revenue growth, and which segment to watch

**Bottom line:** AI adoption and revenue are growing fast and broadly. There's **no standalone "AI" segment** — **Intelligent Cloud (Azure)** is the one to watch. Management **won't put a hard number on ROI**; they describe it as growing confidence plus cost-saving levers, and profit margins are dipping as they build — so the return is still a bet on the future.

- There's no line item called "AI." Microsoft reports just three business segments: Productivity and Business Processes, Intelligent Cloud, and More Personal Computing. The one that captures the AI-and-cloud story is **Intelligent Cloud**, which is where Azure lives `[MD&A L57]`.
- That segment is growing fast: revenue was $39.3 billion, up 32%, with **Azure specifically up 43%** — and Microsoft says it still can't build capacity fast enough to meet demand. It expects Azure to grow about 45% next quarter `[Call L156]` `[Call L157]` `[Call L188]`.
- Usage is climbing quickly across the AI products: the Foundry developer platform has 100,000 customers with revenue more than doubling; Microsoft 365 Copilot passed 30 million paid seats, with new sign-ups more than doubling from the prior quarter; and GitHub Copilot has 50 million users, with its revenue up more than 60% in a single quarter after a pricing change `[Call L66]` `[Call L72]` `[Call L96–98]`.
- On the actual payoff (ROI), a Goldman Sachs analyst asked point-blank how the return on all this spending compares to a year ago. CFO Amy Hood declined to give a number — she said her "math hasn't changed" and pointed instead to growing confidence in the size of the market and in cost-saving levers (better chips, more efficient models). So treat "the returns are good" as management's claim, not a disclosed figure `[Call L277]` `[Call L280–282]`.
- Meanwhile the cost of building is squeezing margins: overall gross margin slipped to 67% and the cloud gross margin to 65%, both down from a year ago, because building AI infrastructure is expensive and more of the sales mix is lower-margin Azure. In short: revenue and usage are clearly up; the profit payoff on the spending isn't proven yet `[Call L134]` `[Call L147]`.

## Question 3 — Major capex, operating expenses, or accounting items to be aware of

**Bottom line:** Capex is enormous and tilted toward short-life chips (flexible, but it pressures margins). **Two accounting moves make reported profit look better** and deserve attention — a change to how long buildings are depreciated, and large, one-off gains from stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic. **Lean on the "adjusted" numbers.**

- Spending on buildings and equipment (capex) was **$41 billion** in the quarter, and about **two-thirds of it went to "short-lived assets"** — meaning chips (CPUs and GPUs) that wear out or get replaced quickly. Microsoft guides to over $50 billion next quarter. The upside of that chip-heavy mix: if demand cools, they can slow the spending fast, since it's the biggest and most flexible piece `[Call L138]` `[Call L199]` `[Call L232]`.
- Operating expenses rose 10% (mostly R&D and talent), but operating margin still ticked up to 45%, and headcount fell 2% `[Call L135]`.
- **Accounting move #1 — depreciation.** Starting next fiscal year, Microsoft will spread the cost of its data centers and office buildings over **25 years instead of 15**. Longer lives mean a smaller depreciation charge each year, which props up future profit. Management calls the benefit "minimal," but it's a real tailwind — and note the current 10-K still lists buildings at just 5 to 15 years `[Call L165]` `[Call L166]` `[Notes L2040–2053]`.
- **Accounting move #2 — investment gains.** Microsoft's reported earnings got a big lift from its ownership stakes in AI companies. This quarter included a **$3.2 billion gain from its Anthropic stake**, and for the full year, gains tied to **OpenAI added $5.0 billion to net income and $0.67 to earnings per share**. These are one-off, non-cash gains that won't repeat on a schedule — which is why Microsoft reports an "adjusted" EPS of $4.74 that strips OpenAI out, and why those adjusted numbers are the cleaner read `[Call L130]` `[Call L131–132]` `[Notes L678]` `[Notes L925]` `[MD&A L997]`.
- Worth keeping in mind: Microsoft openly says it's spending this money "in advance of fully developed revenue streams" — i.e., ahead of the sales that would justify it — and that if it misjudges demand, it could end up writing down (impairing) some of this infrastructure `[MD&A L278]` `[Notes L225]`.
